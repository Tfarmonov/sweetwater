Guide 01 · Beds & groundcover

Mulch vs. pine straw in North Carolina

It's the most common bed question we get, and the honest answer is: it depends on the property, not the product.

Pine straw

Cheaper per application, fast to install, and right at home in the Carolinas. It knits together on slopes better than mulch, and its slight acidity suits azaleas, camellias, and hollies. The catch: it fades to gray in months, so plan on two refreshes a year for a property that gets judged weekly.

Hardwood mulch

Costs more up front but holds color and moisture longer — one annual refresh usually carries a commercial site. It suppresses weeds better at depth and reads as more "finished" at entrances and amenity centers. Keep it to 2–3 inches and off the trunks; mulch volcanoes kill trees slowly and look worse quickly.

What we recommend

Most of our HOA clients run hardwood mulch at entrances and high-visibility beds, pine straw across interior common areas. You get the premium look where it counts and coverage economics everywhere else.

Guide 02 · Irrigation

Irrigation startup and winterization, done right

The two visits that decide whether your system quietly works all year or quietly wastes thousands of gallons.

Spring startup

Open the main slowly — slamming a dry system with full pressure is how fittings crack. Then walk every zone: straighten tilted heads, clear clogged nozzles, check coverage overlap, and reset the controller from winter schedules. A proper startup includes a rain-sensor test; a surprising number have been dead for years.

Fall winterization

The Triangle and even coastal Wilmington get enough hard freezes to split backflow preventers — the single most expensive freeze casualty. Shut off supply, drain or blow out the lines, insulate the backflow, and set the controller to off (not just "rain delay").

Why it's licensed work

Backflow devices protect the drinking-water supply, and North Carolina requires licensed irrigation contractors for a reason. Sweetwater holds an NC irrigation contractor license, so audits, repairs, and seasonal service happen in-house — no subcontractor roulette.

Guide 03 · Fencing

Choosing a fence material that survives NC weather

Humidity, clay soil, and the occasional hurricane are hard on fences. Here's how the materials actually hold up.

Wood

The classic privacy choice and the best look per dollar. Pressure-treated pine runs 12–18 years here if posts are set right; plan on re-staining every 2–3 years. Clay soil heaves — post depth and concrete matter more than the picket brand.

Vinyl

No staining, no rot, and it hoses clean — the maintenance-free option for HOAs. Costs more up front and can get brittle with impact, but modern vinyl handles coastal sun far better than earlier generations.

Chain link & aluminum

Chain link is the workhorse for security and budgets; black-coated versions disappear into landscaping surprisingly well. Aluminum gives the wrought-iron look without the rust — the default for pool enclosures, where code compliance is non-negotiable.

Silt fence

Not decorative, but if you're a builder you already know: installed-to-spec silt fencing is the difference between passing inspection and stop-work orders. Our crews install it start to finish.
